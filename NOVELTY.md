# One-day applied Lean registry lands mostly on occupied ground

The project is novel as a package, but only about a quarter of its roughly 100 results are new. A public Lean 4 precedent was found for most entries, and nearly all of those precedents date from 2026 and are themselves AI-generated or AI-assisted, sitting outside Mathlib. No machine-checked precedent in any proof assistant was found for roughly 20 to 25 results: the Simes family of multiple-testing procedures (Simes, Hochberg, Hommel, Šidák), the Bretz graphical approach with fixed-sequence and fallback, adjusted p-values, Frank–Wolfe, online and projected gradient descent, the AdaBoost training-error bound, Johnson–Lindenstrauss, Körner's trifference bound with T(1..4), the classical secretary rule, and formal statements of the matroid secretary conjecture and the deletion channel. A further handful exist only in Coq or Isabelle (perceptron mistake bound, first welfare theorem) or have a Lean precedent known only from a search snippet (randomized response, Shapley uniqueness). The method, parallel LLM agents with compiler feedback and an LLM statement audit, is the standard 2026 pattern. What no located comparator matches is the scope: six applied fields in one registry, built in one day. The community regards statement fidelity as the hard part, and its benchmark is human domain review of every statement; a Claude-only audit with no human review falls short of that. Every "not found" below is a search result rather than a verified negative: no project was compiled, GitHub code search and several hosts were unavailable, and the project's own statements were not compared with the precedents.

## How to read the evidence: precedents were read, not compiled or compared

The notes behind this report rest on cloned repositories and fetched pages dated 25 November 2026, the same day the project was produced. For the precedent libraries, "proved" means a theorem with a proof is present and a text search found no `sorry`. Nobody compiled the libraries or ran an axiom check. Nobody compared the hypotheses of the project's theorems with those of the precedents, so "already formalized" means "a theorem of this name and general shape exists elsewhere", not "an equivalent statement exists".

Three classes are used throughout. "Lean 4" means at least one public Lean 4 repository contains the result. "Other prover only" means a Coq/Rocq or Isabelle formalization was found but no Lean one. "Nowhere found" means the searches returned nothing in any system.

One fact frames everything else. The largest source of precedent is Meta's `atlas-lean`, generated by the AutoformBot agent pipeline. Its statistics directory alone has 276 files and about 107,000 lines ([atlas-lean](https://github.com/facebookresearch/atlas-lean)). Its first version reported more than 45,000 declarations from 26 textbooks ([arXiv:2605.29955](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29955)). So most of the project's overlap is with other machine-written libraries, not with human-curated ones. Mathlib itself contains almost none of the applied results in any of the four areas.

## Statistics: the Simes family and graphical procedures are the new ground

Most of the project's statistics entries have a Lean 4 precedent in `atlas-lean`, and the newest of these are weeks old. The closed-testing, sufficiency and Rao–Blackwell files were merged on 2 November 2026 ([atlas-lean PR #931](https://github.com/facebookresearch/atlas-lean/pull/931)). Mathlib has none of this. An independent audit states that Mathlib lacks sufficiency, Fisher information, tests, p-values, confidence sets and the Neyman–Pearson lemma ([lean-CKSD MathlibCoverage](https://github.com/bjbraams/lean-CKSD/blob/main/MathlibCoverage.md)). Mathlib's own 1000-theorems list shows no declaration for Glivenko–Cantelli, Rao–Blackwell, Lehmann–Scheffé or Gauss–Markov ([docs/1000.yaml](https://github.com/leanprover-community/mathlib4/blob/master/docs/1000.yaml)).

No result in this area falls in the "other prover only" class. Searches of the Isabelle AFP entry names and web searches for Coq, HOL4, HOL Light and Mizar found none of these inferential results. The nearest non-Lean work is a program logic for hypothesis-testing programs, which verifies annotated programs rather than proving the theorems ([arXiv:2208.07074](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.07074)).

The new statistics content is therefore one coherent block: the Simes inequality and the step-up procedures built on it, Šidák, the graphical framework with its special cases, and adjusted p-values. That block is also where the project's one confirmed defect sits, the Hochberg tie convention, so the newest material is the least cross-checked.

## Optimization, learning theory and privacy: old cores, a few clean firsts

The deterministic first-order core is the oldest and best-vetted precedent in the whole comparison. The Peking University library optlib has gradient descent for convex and strongly convex objectives and Nesterov acceleration, with a peer-reviewed paper behind it ([optlib](https://github.com/optsuite/optlib), [arXiv:2403.11437](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.11437)). Differential privacy has a similarly solid precedent in SampCert, which covers an abstract DP interface, pure DP, zero-concentrated DP and a discrete Laplace sampler ([arXiv:2412.01671](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01671)). The discrete Laplace mechanism corresponds to the project's geometric mechanism.

Learning theory moved fast in 2026. A full fundamental theorem of PAC learning, including sample compression from finite VC dimension, exists in a 21,728-line Lean 4 library that self-reports zero `sorry` ([formal-learning-theory-kernel](https://github.com/Zetetic-Dhruv/formal-learning-theory-kernel)). A multiplicative-weights regret bound was merged into a Lean repository on 24 November 2026, one day before the project, assembled and reviewed only by AI agents ([cslib-frontier PR #8352](https://github.com/cslib-community/cslib-frontier/pull/8352)).

The notes do not say whether the boosting bound is among the formalized claims, so the AdaBoost row is the least safe of the four.

## Coding and information theory: trifference is the standout, the rest is crowded

This is the most crowded area. At least seven public Lean 4 code bases cover classical coding and information theory, several of them agent-generated. One follows Cover and Thomas and states that every proof was written by Claude Code, self-reporting 129,389 lines ([FujiHaruka/information-theory](https://github.com/FujiHaruka/information-theory)). Mathlib remains thin: Hamming distance, Kullback–Leibler divergence with Gibbs' inequality and data processing, and Kraft–McMillan ([Mathlib InformationTheory](https://github.com/leanprover-community/mathlib4/tree/master/Mathlib/InformationTheory)).

Coq's infotheo is the long-standing peer-reviewed precedent for Shannon's theorems, the BSC capacity, and the Singleton and Hamming bounds ([infotheo](https://github.com/affeldt-aist/infotheo)). Here the usual direction is reversed: Gilbert–Varshamov, Plotkin and Fano exist in Lean but were not found in infotheo.

## Method is standard for 2026; breadth and speed are what stand out

Parallel LLM agents working against the Lean compiler, with a separate statement-audit stage, is established practice. LeanMarathon uses contract-scoped agents and stabilizes target fidelity through adversarial review before proving, and formalized seven theorems with 258 lemmas across three autonomous runs ([arXiv:2606.05400](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05400)). DeepMind's AlphaProof Nexus resolved 9 of 353 open Erdős problems at a few hundred dollars each ([arXiv:2605.22763](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.22763v1)). Claude Code specifically was already used to build a roughly 30,000-line statistical learning theory library published at ICML 2026 ([arXiv:2602.02285](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02285)).

The project differs on three axes. It is breadth-first across four broad applied areas, where each comparator is single-domain. It was built in one day, where comparators report weeks to months. And it is framed as a registry of practitioner-facing results. No located source reports a per-day rate, so "100 results in a day" cannot be ranked; given the totals above, it is plausible rather than record-setting.

On the registry framing, Formal Conjectures is the dominant statement registry with 2,615 Lean statements as of May 2026. Its subject mix is led by number theory and combinatorics, and it reports no statistics, optimization or economics category ([arXiv:2605.13171](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.13171v1)). The only applied precedent for hosting open problems is Bei et al.'s library, which has exactly two entries, one of them EFX existence ([OpenProblem.lean](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/gametheoryinlean/EconCSLib/HEAD/EconCSLib/OpenProblem.lean)).

On oversight, the project sits at the low end. Its nearest peers in that respect are `atlas-lean`, where reviewers of the statistics PR could not run the Lean build, and Garg's EconCSLib, whose public status table shows human statement review at 0 of N for nearly every paper ([PAPER_STATUS](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nikhgarg/EconCSLib/HEAD/docs/PAPER_STATUS.md)). The human-reviewed end is represented by the ICML learning-theory library and by an asymptotic-statistics library whose auditor agent traces every hypothesis to its source and whose results are human-audited ([arXiv:2606.20642](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.20642)).

## Statement fidelity is the recognized bottleneck, and LLM-only audits are weak

The community's consistent position in 2026 is that a checked proof says nothing about whether the statement is the intended one. A 41-author survey that includes Terence Tao puts it directly: "checking a formal proof does not by itself establish that it captures the intended problem" ([arXiv:2607.07779](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07779)).

The best quantitative evidence comes from Formal Conjectures. It has fixed **291 misformalizations** against 2,615 statements, with 48% classed as misrepresentation and 35% as semantic errors. Its paper calls stating a problem correctly without a proof "notoriously difficult" ([arXiv:2605.13171](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.13171v1)). Every contribution there requires review by humans with both Lean and domain expertise, and provers are run against submissions to catch trivially true or false statements.

LLM audits are documented as an unreliable substitute. A NeurIPS 2026 study reports that frontier models detect natural statement errors with F1 of 0.29 to 0.52, against 0.74 to 1.00 on synthetic ones ([NeurIPS page](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/168516), search summary only). An earlier study found LLM equivalence judges rating one translator at 97.5% accuracy where humans gave 62.7% ([arXiv:2511.03108](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.03108v1)).

Applied to this project, the audit result of one real defect and about two dozen disclosure gaps should be read as a lower bound on problems, not a clean bill. The risk is uneven. Proved results cannot be false, but they can be weaker than their names suggest. The precedent libraries show exactly this pattern: Gauss–Markov as a matrix inequality, alpha-spending as a union bound, and 20 "reduced cores" in the finance library that prove less than their titles ([arXiv:2606.01356](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01356)). Stated-only entries carry the highest risk. In this project those are the matroid secretary conjecture, the deletion channel, the reliability function and sample compression.

## Venues exist for this work, under disclosure and human-accountability norms

Comparable LLM-assisted applied formalization has appeared at ICML 2026, at the EC'26 workshop on AI-driven research in economics and computation, and on arXiv under cs.AI, cs.GT and cs.LG ([arXiv:2602.02285](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02285), [arXiv:2606.13306](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13306)). The interactive theorem proving venues accept the topic but are wary. ITP 2026 lists sound methods for AI in proof discovery among its topics ([ITP 2026 CFP](https://itp-conference-2026.github.io/cfp)). The CPP 2027 call reportedly notes concern about the volume and quality of AI-assisted submissions ([CPP 2027](https://popl27.sigplan.org/home/CPP-2027), search summary only).

Mathlib is not a realistic destination without expert rework. Its rules require disclosing any AI use in the PR description, labelling substantially LLM-generated PRs, and being able to justify every design decision without AI. They state that code written by AI without supervision of a Lean expert generally does not meet Mathlib's standards ([contribute page source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/leanprover-community/leanprover-community.github.io/lean4/templates/contribute/index.md)).

More natural homes exist for parts of the project. Formal Conjectures accepts AI-assisted contributions under mandatory human review, which suits the open-problem statements ([arXiv:2605.13171](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.13171v1)). Statlib, the community statistics library, requires a design issue before code ([Statlib](https://github.com/stat-lib/statlib)); its current scope is divergences, testing lower bounds and e-variables, so the multiple-testing block would complement it. Lean Pool positions itself as an archive grown and maintained by AI agents ([arXiv:2609.25199](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25199)).

The shared norms are disclosure of model and workflow, human authors who take responsibility, and no AI listed as author. The Mizar journal states the last explicitly ([Formalized Mathematics policy](https://mizar.uwb.edu.pl/fm/Editorial_Policy.html)). A bulk release without per-item vetting has already drawn public criticism this year, in the reaction to OpenAI's October 2026 release of several hundred results; that account rests on secondary coverage only ([Forklog](https://forklog.com/en/news/mathematicians-criticize-openais-approach-to-publishing-proofs)).

## What could not be verified

The limits below bear directly on the novelty assessment.

- **No statement comparison.** The project's Lean statements were not read against any precedent. Overlap is judged by result name and general shape.
- **No builds or axiom checks.** No precedent library was compiled; "no `sorry`" is a text search or the project's own claim. For `atlas-lean`, use of `native_decide`, custom axioms or weakened statements was not checked.
- **No file-level dates.** Shallow clones gave only head-commit dates, so who was first for a given theorem is mostly unknown. The project and several precedents are days or weeks apart.
- **Search coverage.** GitHub code search was unavailable. The Lean Zulip archive, Reservoir, the Mathlib docs site, the Isabelle AFP site and the Statlib roadmap were unreachable. AFP was checked by entry names only; Coq, HOL and Mizar by web search only. Small or unindexed repositories would be missed.
- **Unread files that could change a verdict.** These include `atlas-lean`'s `HierarchicalTesting.lean` (graphical procedures) and its trees outside the directories searched; ProofLoom's task list (SGD, Frank–Wolfe); SampCert's approximate-DP file; the contents of Garg's Freund–Schapire formalization (AdaBoost); and the Kelly theorem in formal-mathfin.
- **Snippet-only precedents.** The Lean Shapley characterization, the ICML Lean paper with randomized response, the Lean companion to the Pareto secretary paper, the Isabelle first welfare theorem and VCG entries, and the NeurIPS fidelity numbers were not opened.
- **Results not searched.** Weighted Bonferroni was not searched for at all. For the matroid secretary conjecture, Formal Conjectures could not be searched directly.
- **Conflicting or implausible figures.** The 986,391-line figure for Garg's EconCSLib and the counts for OpenAI's October release are unreconciled across sources and are not relied on here.
- **Project facts taken as given.** The entry list, the one-day timeline and the audit outcome come from the brief and were not checked against the repository.

## Conclusion

The defensible claim is narrow and specific: the first applied, cross-domain registry of this breadth, containing what appear to be the first machine-checked treatments in any prover of the Simes-family and graphical multiple-testing procedures, trifferent codes, and a handful of classical results (Frank–Wolfe, online gradient descent, Johnson–Lindenstrauss, the secretary rule). A general "first Lean formalization" claim is not defensible, and for roughly three-quarters of the entries a precedent exists. The multiple-testing block is the most valuable part, because it is a connected body of practitioner-relevant theory that the largest AI-generated library explicitly left out, and it fits an existing community library's scope.

Two things would change this assessment more than any further literature search. The first is a file-level comparison of the two dozen candidate firsts against `atlas-lean`, ProofLoom's library and GitHub code search, repeated immediately before any public claim, since relevant precedents were merged the day before the project. The second is human domain review of those same statements. Because the precedents are themselves largely unreviewed machine output, a small set of human-audited statements would differentiate the project more than the count of 100 does.
